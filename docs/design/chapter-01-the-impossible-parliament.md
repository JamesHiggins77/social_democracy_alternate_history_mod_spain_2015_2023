# Chapter I — The Impossible Parliament
## Spain, December 2015–February 2016

**Status:** Chapter design and implementation brief  
**Playable span:** December 2015–February 2016  
**Player character:** Pedro Sánchez, Secretary-General of the Spanish Socialist Workers' Party (PSOE)

## 1. Chapter purpose

The player enters immediately after the general election of 20 December 2015. The PSOE has finished second behind Mariano Rajoy's People's Party (PP), but the election has produced no straightforward governing majority. Podemos and its regional allies, Ciudadanos and several territorial parties hold the balance.

Sánchez must decide what the PSOE is for in the new parliament: a party that facilitates a PP government, an opposition party seeking to replace Rajoy, or the centre of an alternative government. Every route carries a cost. The party is divided over strategy, and Sánchez's authority is real but not unlimited.

This chapter introduces parliamentary arithmetic, investiture, PSOE factions, regional power-brokers, coalition negotiations, advisers, public opinion and the distinction between Sánchez's personal authority and the institutional strength of the PSOE.

The historical crisis is the starting pressure, not a pre-written sequence of player decisions.

## 2. Historical starting point

The 20 December 2015 election returned a fragmented Congress. The PP won 123 seats, the PSOE 90, Podemos and its regional alliances 69, and Ciudadanos 40. Smaller territorial and regional parties held the remaining seats. No party had the 176 seats required for an absolute majority.

These are the historical baseline figures, not mandatory results in every playthrough. Store the election result as the initial parliamentary state; later elections must be allowed to diverge.

The King consults party leaders and proposes Rajoy as candidate for the presidency of the government. Rajoy's difficulty securing sufficient support creates an opening for Sánchez, but not an automatic path to power.

**Opening situation report**

- **PP:** largest parliamentary group; Rajoy is the incumbent prime minister.
- **PSOE:** second-largest group; Sánchez is the principal alternative leader of a national party.
- **Podemos:** wants a change of government and is open to negotiations, but its demands and relationship with the PSOE are contentious.
- **Ciudadanos:** willing to negotiate on some reforms, but strongly opposed to a government dependent on Podemos.
- **Territorial parties:** potentially decisive, but Catalan, Basque, Canarian and other regional interests are not interchangeable.
- **PSOE organisation:** Sánchez has formal leadership, while regional leaders, former secretaries-general and senior figures retain influence.

## 3. Opening scene — The morning after

**Scene intent:** Establish the result, Sánchez's personal stakes and the fact that every route is politically dangerous.

The player receives a private briefing from the senior team. The election has weakened the old two-party system. The PSOE has avoided the collapse some predicted, but it has not won. The party faces two competing accusations: that it will rescue the PP, or that it will hand the country to Podemos.

The opening choice establishes the player's initial posture. It changes relationships and public expectations but does not lock the player into a route.

### Opening posture choices

1. **The responsible opposition.** Refuse to facilitate a PP government, but avoid promising an alternative before the numbers are known. This keeps options open and reassures anti-PP voters, but risks accusations of indecision.
2. **An alternative government.** Announce that the PSOE will explore a change of government and begin talks with Podemos and other groups. This signals initiative and may energise members, but raises expectations and alarms moderates, regional presidents and Ciudadanos.
3. **Institutional stability.** Prioritise governability and explore whether a limited agreement with the PP is possible. This reassures some institutional voices, but risks a backlash from members and voters opposed to Rajoy and gives internal opponents grounds to accuse Sánchez of preparing to abstain.

Changing posture later should be possible, but politically costly.

## 4. The parliamentary board

Represent Congress as a live negotiation problem, not a single coalition slider.

| Group | Historical seats | Opening role |
|---|---:|---|
| PP | 123 | Largest group; Rajoy seeks investiture |
| PSOE | 90 | Sánchez's parliamentary group |
| Podemos and regional alliances | 69 | Potential alternative-government partner |
| Ciudadanos | 40 | Potential reform partner; rejects dependence on Podemos |
| ERC | 9 | Catalan republican group |
| Democràcia i Llibertat | 8 | Catalan nationalist group |
| EAJ-PNV | 6 | Basque nationalist group |
| Unidad Popular–IU | 2 | Left-wing group |
| EH Bildu | 2 | Basque left-nationalist group |
| Coalición Canaria | 1 | Canarian regional group |

This is a simplified political ledger. Store parliamentary groups, electoral alliances, parties and individual MPs separately where necessary. Do not assume every member of a multi-party alliance votes identically.

### Investiture rules

- In the first ballot, a candidate needs an absolute majority of the full Congress: 176 votes.
- If the first ballot fails, a second ballot takes place 48 hours later. The candidate then needs more votes in favour than against.
- Abstentions do not count as votes against in the second ballot. Abstention can therefore be decisive without constituting an affirmative coalition agreement.
- A failed investiture does not install the opposition leader. The King continues consultations and may propose another candidate.
- If no candidate obtains Congress's confidence within the constitutional period following the first investiture vote, the Cortes are dissolved and a new election is called.

Distinguish a formal coalition, legislative agreement, confidence-and-supply arrangement, issue-by-issue understanding and abstention. They have different political and parliamentary consequences.

## 5. First adviser circle

Advisers are political actors, not interchangeable stat bonuses. Each has an outlook, relationship with Sánchez, network and personal ambitions.

### Core advisers and negotiators

- **Antonio Hernando:** PSOE parliamentary spokesperson; central parliamentary negotiator, attentive to arithmetic and institutional positioning.
- **Rodolfo Ares:** experienced political operator with Basque political experience; useful for regional and cross-party negotiations.
- **José Enrique Serrano:** institutional and constitutional expertise; suited to negotiating the architecture and wording of agreements.
- **Meritxell Batet:** policy and programme expertise, federalist constitutional outlook and links to the PSC.
- **María Luisa Carcedo:** social policy and public-health expertise; voice for the party's social programme.
- **Jordi Sevilla:** economic policy adviser with a more moderate economic profile; useful in talks with Ciudadanos and business-facing interlocutors.

The player should not necessarily be able to appoint all six to every negotiating team. Availability, trust and internal standing change with decisions.

### Adviser mechanics

Each adviser has:
1. Competence in relevant tasks (negotiation, communication, policy, organisation).
2. Trust in Sánchez, affected by consultation and appointments.
3. A multi-dimensional political outlook, not one left-right score.
4. Personal ambition, including desired party or government roles.
5. A network of MPs, regional leaders, unions and external parties.

Advisers may disagree privately, leak concerns, refuse assignments or make their own contacts. Disagreement is not automatically betrayal: professional disagreement and personal loyalty can coexist.

## 6. PSOE internal politics

The PSOE is not a single actor. Introduce competing networks without assigning every politician a permanent faction label.

- **Sánchez's leadership team:** people whose positions or influence derive from his 2014 victory. César Luena, as Secretary of Organisation, is especially important.
- **Institutional and regional leadership:** autonomous-community presidents, regional secretaries-general and senior officials concerned with governability, electoral risk and their own regional mandates.
- **Left and labour networks:** MPs, activists and union-linked figures wary of facilitating a PP government and more open to cooperation with Podemos.
- **Federalist and territorial reformers:** figures favouring constitutional reform and recognition of territorial diversity, but not necessarily independence or a referendum.
- **Former national leadership and elders:** Felipe González, José Luis Rodríguez Zapatero and Alfredo Pérez Rubalcaba. They have influence, but no formal right to direct Sánchez.

Networks overlap. A regional president may favour federal reform while opposing a Podemos agreement; a moderate economic voice may support Sánchez personally; a left-wing MP may distrust his negotiating tactics. Track relationships person by person.

**Initial pressure points**

- Should the PSOE vote against Rajoy, abstain or negotiate another arrangement?
- Is Podemos a potential partner, an electoral rival, or both?
- Can an agreement with Ciudadanos be reconciled with Podemos's demands?
- Should regional leaders have a veto over national strategy?
- Does Sánchez consult the Federal Executive, parliamentary group, regional presidents or party members first?
- Is the priority to form a government, preserve party unity or prepare for another election?

## 7. Core decision cards

Cards may appear in a different order depending on previous choices and the evolving situation.

### Card A — Rajoy asks for support
**Trigger:** Rajoy is nominated for investiture.

Options: vote against Rajoy; abstain where procedurally available; negotiate a limited agreement with the PP; or demand a different candidate and further consultations.

Consequences affect Sánchez's credibility, member confidence, relations with regional leaders and the likelihood of another election. Abstention is not a neutral action.

### Card B — The Podemos question
**Trigger:** The PSOE explores an alternative government.

Options: begin formal talks with Pablo Iglesias; explore a programme agreement without committing to coalition; insist on a PSOE-led government and negotiate external support; or reject a Podemos-dependent government and seek another route.

Podemos's demands must arise from its own priorities and strategic incentives. Iglesias is an independent political actor, not a dialogue tree that always accepts or rejects the same offer.

### Card C — The Ciudadanos channel
**Trigger:** Ciudadanos signals willingness to discuss reforms.

Options: open bilateral talks; explore a three-party arrangement with Podemos; seek a narrower reform pact; or refuse talks because Ciudadanos is incompatible with the PSOE's programme.

A PSOE–Ciudadanos agreement must not automatically produce a majority. Viability depends on other groups' positions and the votes or abstentions available.

### Card D — The regional presidents
**Trigger:** National strategy threatens regional interests or party unity.

Options: convene regional presidents; consult the Federal Executive first; announce a strategy before consultation; or offer regional leaders a formal role in negotiations.

Regional presidents have distinct interests and leverage. Their responses depend on their relationship with Sánchez, regional electoral position and view of the proposed agreement.

### Card E — What does the party promise?
**Trigger:** Negotiations move from procedure to policy.

The player selects a limited number of priorities:
- employment and labour rights;
- pensions and social protection;
- education and public services;
- anti-corruption and institutional reform;
- constitutional and territorial reform;
- economic and fiscal policy.

The programme creates trade-offs. A proposal that improves relations with one potential partner may alienate another or divide the PSOE.

## 8. Monthly structure

Use monthly turns by default, with additional decision stages for major events such as an investiture vote or leadership crisis.

Each month:
1. **Situation report:** parliamentary arithmetic, polling, public approval, economy, faction balance and negotiations.
2. **Adviser consultation:** hear a limited number of advisers; advice may conflict.
3. **Political actions:** choose a small number of actions, such as negotiating, campaigning, consulting party bodies, managing relationships or developing policy.
4. **Events:** resolve scheduled and conditional events, media stories and actions by independent political actors.
5. **Monthly resolution:** update party support, faction influence, relationships, parliamentary commitments and public opinion.

A major investiture vote interrupts the ordinary loop and uses a dedicated sequence of negotiation, public positioning, vote and aftermath.

## 9. Chapter progression and exit conditions

The chapter begins in December 2015 and should normally reach its conclusion by the end of February 2016. It can conclude earlier if a decisive parliamentary outcome occurs.

Possible outcomes:
- **Rajoy survives:** the PSOE chooses to abstain or otherwise permits a PP government, with consequences for internal opposition and public support.
- **Alternative-government negotiations advance:** Sánchez builds a credible route to investiture but must reconcile partners with incompatible demands.
- **Deadlock continues:** no candidate secures confidence; the risk of a repeat election rises.
- **Unexpected agreement:** the player finds a viable parliamentary route not taken historically. Support it if the numbers and political commitments justify it.

Do not force the historical March 2016 failed investiture in this chapter. It belongs to the next chapter if the deadlock remains unresolved.

The closing report summarises who has the initiative in Congress; which parties will negotiate with Sánchez; PSOE internal unity and the strongest opposing network; Sánchez's personal authority and public standing; the likelihood of a new election; and unresolved promises or private tensions that can return later.

## 10. Implementation guidance

This is a design specification, not yet a scene-code implementation. Before converting it into scenes:

- replace inherited Weimar/German party and demographic identifiers with consistent Spanish identifiers;
- centralise the seat ledger and vote rules so all cards use the same arithmetic;
- separate party, parliamentary group, alliance and individual MP data;
- represent people and regional organisations as independent actors with relationships and incentives;
- make historical dates event opportunities, not unconditional commands;
- ensure displayed statistics are backed by actual game variables;
- test every investiture route, including abstentions, defections and failed votes.

**Out of scope:** the September–October 2016 leadership confrontation, the 2017 PSOE primary and Sánchez's return to the leadership. Those belong in later chapters and should be foreshadowed here only through relationships, faction influence and unresolved strategic disagreements.

## Historical reference points for implementation

- Official Congress of Deputies results for the 20 December 2015 general election.
- Spanish Constitution, Article 99 (investiture and dissolution following failure to form a government).
- Spanish Constitution, Article 113 (constructive vote of no confidence; relevant to later chapters).
- PSOE Federal Executive elected at the 2014 party congress.
- Contemporary reporting on the PSOE negotiating team and January–February 2016 investiture negotiations.

Historical figures and procedural details should be checked against official election and constitutional records during implementation. Preserve the distinction between the historical baseline and player-created outcomes.


## 11. Lessons from the original Social Democracy game: advisers and cards

This section is based on inspection of the original game's Dendry scene files, especially `main.scene.dry`, representative files in `source/scenes/advisors/` and `source/scenes/party_affairs/`, `events/black_thursday.scene.dry`, and `post_event.scene.dry`. The original source is available in the [aucchen/social_democracy_alternate_history repository](https://github.com/aucchen/social_democracy_alternate_history).

### 11.1 The main screen and the role of pinned advisers

The original game's main scene is effectively a political desk. It presents separate decks for Party Affairs and Government Affairs, alongside pinned adviser cards. The main scene uses `is-hand: true`, a defined hand capacity, and deck scenes such as `@party` and `@govt`. The adviser area is not simply another random deck: advisers are persistent cards that remain visible and can be opened whenever the player wants to consult or use their abilities.

Each adviser has an individual scene, normally in `source/scenes/advisors/`, with a structure such as:

- `title`: the person's name;
- `is-pinned-card: true`: keeps the character available on the main screen;
- `tags: advisor` plus relevant political/faction tags;
- `card-image`: the person's portrait;
- `view-if`: determines whether the character is currently available;
- `new-page: true`: opens a dedicated adviser page;
- narrative biography and a list of selectable actions.

The adviser page is a small menu of actions, not one generic button. Marie Juchacz, for example, has distinct actions for emergency fundraising, Workers' Welfare and organising women. Wladimir Woytinsky can build support for an economic plan, help carry out an adopted plan, or campaign during the depression. Each action has its own conditions, effects and explanatory text.

**Design implication for Spain:** Sánchez's inner circle should be represented by persistent, individually authored adviser cards. Opening an adviser should reveal several distinct ways to use that person: negotiate with a particular party or leader, prepare a policy proposal, consult a regional network, manage the parliamentary group, brief the press, or calm an internal dispute. Not every adviser should be useful for every task. Their political outlook, relationships and access should determine which actions are available and what risks accompany them.

The original system also demonstrates that advisers can be made available conditionally. A character or role can appear only when the relevant political circumstances exist. For the Spanish game, an adviser might become unavailable after resignation, dismissal, a falling-out, a change of office or a loss of access to Sánchez. A person who is not a formal adviser may still appear as an event actor or negotiator.

### 11.2 Adviser actions: conditions, costs, cooldowns and effects

An adviser action is authored as a sub-scene beneath the adviser's main scene. The original examples use:

- `title` and `subtitle` to explain the action and its likely cost;
- `choose-if` to determine whether the action can be selected;
- `unavailable-subtitle` to explain why it cannot currently be used;
- `view-if` to hide actions that are irrelevant or no longer possible;
- `on-arrival` to apply its effects;
- `go-to` to transfer the player into a more detailed event or policy sequence when needed.

Many original adviser actions share a global `advisor_action_timer`. A successful action sets the timer (often to six months in the original game), and other actions display how long remains before another adviser action can be taken. This makes choosing which adviser to use a strategic decision rather than allowing the player to exhaust every character's abilities in one turn.

The actions are not all simple numerical bonuses. They can be conditional on holding a ministry, being in government, having sufficient resources, reaching a threshold of faction support, or having an earlier event occur. Woytinsky's plan-adoption action, for instance, depends on the economic crisis, support for the plan and factional conditions. Juchacz's welfare action requires resources and changes both the organisation's capacity and support among relevant social groups.

**Design implication for Spain:** use a shared adviser-action opportunity or a deliberately limited number of adviser consultations per month, but avoid copying the original six-month cooldown mechanically. A monthly political simulation needs a cadence appropriate to its scale. A useful approach would be to distinguish:
- **Consultation:** hear advice or obtain information; relatively frequent, but limited by the number of meetings or available time.
- **Personal intervention:** ask an adviser to spend political capital, make calls, negotiate or manage a faction; less frequent and potentially risky.
- **Formal appointment or delegation:** assign someone a continuing responsibility, changing their availability and influence over time.

Actions should have visible requirements and a clear explanation when unavailable. Their effects should be multi-dimensional: a successful negotiation may improve one relationship while angering a PSOE faction, raising expectations among a partner, or weakening Sánchez's control of the process. Avoid making every adviser action a flat increase to a single Sánchez or PSOE statistic.

### 11.3 Ordinary cards: one card, a meaningful political problem

The original Party Affairs cards are temporary opportunities drawn from a deck. A typical card such as Political Rally or Media has `is-card: true`, a `tags` category, a `card-image`, eligibility conditions such as `view-if`, and an `on-arrival` effect that can consume an action or start a timer. The player then chooses among several sub-scenes.

The Political Rally card is a particularly useful example. It does not simply say “gain support”. It asks what the rally should be about: class struggle, welfare, democracy, pacifism, an economic plan, or local concerns. Each option has different effects on different voter groups and party factions. Some options become available only if the party has adopted the relevant policy. The choice also has a narrative explanation of who is likely to respond and why.

The Media card offers a different set of choices: commercialise newspapers, focus on campaigning, allow ideological debate, or invest in radio. These choices involve trade-offs between resources, electoral reach and internal factional satisfaction. The Choosing Our Enemies card is even more explicitly strategic: choosing to attack the KPD, the far right, the NSDAP alone or the bourgeois parties affects faction strength, relations with other parties and the possibility of future cooperation.

These examples establish a strong pattern:
1. A card introduces a recognisable political situation or opportunity.
2. Its choices are concrete strategies, not merely differently worded stat boosts.
3. Each choice changes several connected parts of the simulation.
4. The narrative explains the political logic and likely consequences.
5. Conditions make some choices dependent on previous decisions or the current situation.

**Design implication for Spain:** cards should represent real political work and dilemmas: a negotiating offer arrives, a regional president demands a meeting, an interview request creates a communications choice, a leaked conversation threatens the leadership, or the parliamentary group disputes the line on investiture. Choices should make the player decide what Sánchez does, not simply select a generic “left”, “centre” or “right” bonus.

### 11.4 Card availability, decks and the difference between cards and events

The original separates card categories into decks. In `main.scene.dry`, Party Affairs and Government Affairs are represented as decks, with Government Affairs only appearing when the relevant time or government conditions are met. Individual cards can be gated by `view-if`, timers and other conditions. Cards can also have visit limits, and their sub-scenes can offer a discard, return or cancellation route.

The original also has event scenes, which are not identical to ordinary action cards. The Black Thursday event, for example, has a date-based trigger, an event tag, a one-visit limit and a `face-image`. Its arrival applies the immediate economic shock and presents a narrative event with responses. By contrast, an ordinary card such as Political Rally uses `card-image` and consumes a player action to pursue a chosen activity.

For the Spanish mod, preserve this distinction:
- **Action cards:** things Sánchez and the PSOE can choose to do, such as negotiate, campaign, consult, organise or prepare a policy.
- **Event cards:** developments that happen to the player, such as a public statement by another leader, a court ruling, a leak, an election result, a resignation or a parliamentary defeat. The player responds, but does not choose whether the event occurs.
- **Pinned character cards:** people who remain accessible and offer a set of possible interventions or advice.
- **Major crisis sequences:** multi-stage scenes that temporarily take priority over the ordinary card loop, such as an investiture vote, a Federal Committee confrontation or a leadership challenge.

Do not make every historical occurrence an action card. A rival's public intervention should generally be an event or an action by an independent actor, not a card the player “plays” on their behalf.

### 11.5 The monthly action economy and post-event resolution

The original's action system is connected to a central post-event scene. Cards and actions increment a monthly action counter; the post-event resolution then updates the date and decrements timers when the relevant action threshold has been reached. It also recalculates derived statistics and normalises values. This is important: individual cards apply immediate changes, but the post-event scene is responsible for the shared consequences of taking an action and moving the simulation forward.

This creates a predictable rhythm. The player can see a card, make a choice, receive its immediate narrative and mechanical result, and then return to the main screen. The central resolution handles the consequences that should apply consistently after actions, rather than requiring every card to implement its own version of time advancement.

**Design implication for Spain:** implement one central monthly-resolution routine. It should handle the calendar, polling updates, faction and relationship drift, negotiation deadlines, media cycles, adviser availability and scheduled events. Individual cards should change the relevant variables and set flags or deadlines; they should not each independently advance the month or duplicate the same monthly calculations.

The Spanish game may need a more flexible action budget than the original. For example, the player might receive a small number of major political actions each month, while meetings, statements and urgent events use separate limits. Investiture votes and leadership crises should interrupt or expand the ordinary action sequence rather than being squeezed into a normal card.

### 11.6 What to borrow — and what not to copy

**Borrow from the original:**
- Persistent adviser cards with individual biographies, portraits and multiple distinct actions.
- Clear availability requirements and explanatory unavailable text.
- A limited action economy that forces prioritisation.
- Cards built around concrete political dilemmas and meaningful alternatives.
- Conditional card availability, timers, visit limits and flags that allow earlier decisions to shape later events.
- Separate action cards, event scenes and character/adviser cards.
- A central post-action/monthly resolution routine.
- Immediate narrative feedback explaining why a choice helped one group and harmed another.

**Adapt rather than copy:**
- The original's six-month shared adviser cooldown may be too slow for the intense December 2015–October 2016 period. Use a smaller monthly action budget and differentiated cooldowns where appropriate.
- The original's large deck of recurring policy and campaigning actions suits a long-running party-management game. Spain's opening chapter should initially prioritise negotiations, parliamentary arithmetic, party consultation and leadership management.
- The original's faction and voter-group statistics are designed for the SPD and Weimar electoral system. Do not transplant those variables directly. Spain needs distinct measures for PSOE factional balance, regional organisation, parliamentary discipline, leader authority, partner trust and public approval.
- The original often expresses political outcomes through numerical changes. Retain the readable narrative explanations, but make important Spanish political actors capable of refusing, counter-offering, delaying, leaking or pursuing their own strategies.

### 11.7 Proposed Spanish implementation pattern

For Chapter I, organise the main screen around three persistent areas:

1. **People:** pinned adviser and key-actor cards. Sánchez's core team is accessible here; other figures can appear as contacts, rivals or temporary interlocutors when relevant.
2. **Political work:** a small set of action decks, initially focused on parliamentary negotiations, party organisation and public communication. Government policy actions should become more prominent only if the player forms a government.
3. **Events and crises:** date-triggered or condition-triggered developments, with major sequences taking control when an investiture or party confrontation requires several linked decisions.

Each adviser should have a concise character page and a small number of authored actions. Each action should specify its eligibility, cost, time commitment, immediate effects, delayed consequences and any actors whose reactions it may trigger. Each ordinary card should present a specific situation, a limited set of plausible choices and consequences that flow into later scenes.

For the first prototype, implement only a few complete examples rather than a large number of shallow cards:
- one adviser card with several distinct interventions;
- one negotiation action card with multiple partner-specific approaches;
- one PSOE consultation or regional-leaders card;
- one event card that can trigger independently of the player's action;
- one multi-stage investiture sequence;
- one monthly-resolution routine that consistently updates time and deadlines.

This will test the original game's proven scene architecture while ensuring the Spanish mod's mechanics are built around Sánchez's actual political problem rather than a reskinned Weimar card deck.


## 12. Spanish adviser roster organised by PSOE faction

The Spanish version should follow the original game's faction-organised adviser structure. In the Weimar game, advisers are not presented as one undifferentiated cabinet of experts: each pinned character card carries a faction tag (for example `left`, `labor` or `centrist`), and the adviser roster makes the party's internal balance visible. The Spanish mod should do the same. The player should be able to see who represents each current of the PSOE, what their priorities are, and which political resources they can bring to bear.

The factions below are **simulation groupings**, not claims that the PSOE had six formally constituted, disciplined blocs in 2015. They overlap, contain internal disagreements and can change their alignment. A politician's starting tag describes their strongest political network or tendency at the opening date, not an immutable identity. Some important figures should remain independent actors rather than being forced into a faction.

### 12.1 Sanchistas — Sánchez's leadership network

**Political centre:** defend Sánchez's mandate from the 2014 primary, consolidate the national leadership and preserve room for a new political strategy.

**Core figures and advisers**
- **César Luena** — Secretary of Organisation and one of Sánchez's closest organisational allies at the opening. Access to the party machine, federal organisation and internal appointments.
- **Adriana Lastra** — trusted parliamentary and party operator; useful for membership relations, parliamentary discipline and internal mobilisation.
- **José Luis Ábalos** — initially part of the broader Sánchez network, with strong organisational and territorial skills; his prominence and role can grow substantially in later chapters.
- **Óscar Puente** — municipal and communications ally; useful for public messaging and building a political profile beyond the national apparatus.
- **María José Limón / Susana Sumelzo** — Sumelzo is a useful national-level ally and intermediary, particularly for territorial relationships; availability and influence should reflect the date and later developments rather than being assumed to hold the same office throughout.

**Distinctive adviser actions:** secure support in the Federal Executive; count votes in the Federal Committee; mobilise supportive provincial organisations; coordinate parliamentary messaging; defend Sánchez publicly; identify wavering members before a leadership challenge.

**Internal tensions:** loyalty to Sánchez does not mean agreement on every tactical decision. Organisational control, parliamentary strategy and the balance between consultation and presidential leadership can divide this network.

### 12.2 Institutional social democrats and parliamentary pragmatists

**Political centre:** protect the PSOE's credibility as a governing party, maintain institutional influence and seek workable parliamentary agreements. This is a tendency, not a single unified faction; it includes people who may disagree sharply over whether to facilitate a PP government.

**Core figures and advisers**
- **Antonio Hernando** — parliamentary spokesperson and a central figure in negotiations. Particularly important for parliamentary arithmetic, group discipline and contacts with other parties.
- **José Enrique Serrano** — institutional and constitutional strategist; suited to drafting agreements, assessing investiture procedure and negotiating the architecture of a government.
- **Jordi Sevilla** — economic-policy voice and interlocutor for centrist, business-facing and Ciudadanos-compatible proposals.
- **Alfredo Pérez Rubalcaba** — former secretary-general and senior institutional voice; better treated as an influential elder and independent broker than as a routine operational adviser.
- **Elena Valenciano** — experienced national figure with European and international links; can provide political and communications advice where appropriate.

**Distinctive adviser actions:** test the viability of a parliamentary agreement; negotiate procedural guarantees; prepare an investiture strategy; assess the electoral and institutional cost of abstention; broker compromises with centrist parties.

**Internal tensions:** pragmatists may disagree about whether institutional responsibility requires abstention, whether a Podemos agreement is viable, and how much policy compromise is acceptable. Do not make this group automatically pro-PP or anti-Sánchez.

### 12.3 Regional barons and federal pragmatists

**Political centre:** defend the autonomy, electoral interests and governing credibility of the PSOE's regional federations. Their shared interest is influence over national strategy; they are not a unified ideological bloc.

**Core figures and power-brokers**
- **Susana Díaz (Andalusia)** — the most consequential potential rival to Sánchez in this period; commands a major federation and has her own national ambitions. A rival power centre, not simply an adviser who can be summoned at will.
- **Javier Fernández (Asturias)** — institutional and organisational heavyweight; can become a key broker in a leadership or Federal Committee crisis.
- **Emiliano García-Page (Castilla-La Mancha)** — regional president with a strong interest in protecting his government's room for manoeuvre and a cautious approach to Podemos.
- **Guillermo Fernández Vara (Extremadura)** — regional leader with institutional priorities and influence in federal debates.
- **Ximo Puig (Valencia)** — regional president operating in a plural coalition environment; brings a distinct perspective on territorial and coalition politics.
- **Javier Lambán (Aragón)** — regional president whose position can diverge from Sánchez's national strategy.
- **Other regional counterweights:** Francina Armengol (Balearic Islands), Idoia Mendia (Basque Country), Luis Tudanca (Castile and León), María Chivite (Navarre) and Miquel Iceta (PSC) should be modelled as consequential regional actors, with their own relationships and changing positions.

**Distinctive adviser/interlocutor actions:** convene regional presidents; negotiate a common federal position; count regional delegates; secure or withhold public endorsements; coordinate territorial demands; mediate between national leadership and autonomous-community governments.

**Implementation rule:** most regional barons should not be permanently pinned as friendly advisers. They should be represented as faction-linked power-broker cards or contacts that become available for consultation when the player has sufficient trust or a relevant issue is active. Their support can be conditional, transactional or openly hostile. Susana Díaz and Javier Fernández in particular should have independent agendas.

### 12.4 The social-democratic left and labour movement

**Political centre:** defend social-democratic economic and labour priorities, resist an overly centrist accommodation, and maintain the PSOE's relationship with organised labour and its activist base. This group is not identical to Podemos and should not automatically favour a coalition with it.

**Core figures and advisers**
- **María Luisa Carcedo** — social-policy expertise and a voice for public services and welfare priorities.
- **Carmen Montón** — progressive policy and public-health experience; useful on social policy and the party's reform agenda.
- **Manuel de la Rocha Rubí** — economic-policy adviser with a more socially interventionist profile; useful for employment, fiscal and social investment proposals.
- **Óscar López** — experienced party organiser and political operator; his relationships and loyalties should be modelled rather than assumed.
- **UGT leadership and union interlocutors** — represent labour's institutional interests as an external but closely connected power centre. Do not collapse the unions into the PSOE faction system.

**Distinctive adviser actions:** develop employment and welfare proposals; consult UGT; mobilise activists and affiliated networks; assess the effect of a policy compromise on working-class voters; build support for a more ambitious social programme.

**Internal tensions:** the labour movement, parliamentary left and younger activists do not necessarily share the same view of Podemos, coalition government or party discipline. Keep union leaders as independent actors who can support, oppose or bargain with the PSOE leadership.

### 12.5 Constitutional federalists and territorial reformers

**Political centre:** reform the territorial settlement, defend a plural conception of Spain and pursue constitutional change while maintaining a commitment to the constitutional framework. This tendency overlaps with regional pragmatists and institutional social democrats but deserves a distinct policy voice.

**Core figures and advisers**
- **Meritxell Batet** — federalist constitutional reformer and an important link to the PSC; central for drafting a territorial and constitutional offer.
- **Miquel Iceta** — PSC leader and a major interlocutor on Catalonia; should also have his own regional and party interests, rather than being reduced to an adviser tag.
- **Patxi López** — experienced former Lehendakari with federal and institutional credentials; potentially useful as a mediator across regional and national networks.
- **Francesc Vallès and other PSC figures** may be used as supporting actors if the later scope of the game requires a deeper Catalan network.

**Distinctive adviser actions:** draft a federal constitutional proposal; negotiate with PSC leadership; consult regional federations; prepare a response to Catalan demands; seek cross-party support for institutional reform.

**Internal tensions:** federal reformers may disagree over the exact constitutional model, the scope of Catalan self-government and whether a referendum can be part of a negotiated settlement. They are not automatically aligned with the regional barons or with Podemos.

### 12.6 The old guard and former national leadership

**Political centre:** preserve the PSOE's institutional legacy, influence national strategy and protect the party's established networks. This is an informal circle of influential former leaders, not a formal faction with a unified programme.

**Core figures**
- **Felipe González** — former prime minister and highly influential elder; can intervene publicly or privately, but should not be treated as a routine adviser subordinate to Sánchez.
- **José Luis Rodríguez Zapatero** — former prime minister with his own relationships and views on dialogue, territorial issues and party strategy.
- **Alfredo Pérez Rubalcaba** — former secretary-general with strong institutional knowledge; can be an adviser, mediator or critic depending on the situation.
- **Other former senior office-holders** can appear in events, private consultations and public interventions as the timeline advances.

**Distinctive interventions:** private counsel; public endorsement or criticism; contact with business, media and international networks; mediation during a party crisis; warnings about electoral or institutional risks.

**Implementation rule:** these figures should normally be independent senior actors, not ordinary pinned advisers with repeatable stat bonuses. Their influence comes from reputation, networks and the ability to shape the debate. They may help Sánchez, pressure him or undermine him.

### 12.7 How faction organisation should work in the game

Use faction tags on adviser scenes in the same way the original uses tags such as `left`, `labor` and `centrist`. Suggested Spanish tags:

- `sanchista`
- `institutionalist`
- `regionalist`
- `social_left`
- `federalist`
- `old_guard`

Characters can carry more than one tag where genuinely appropriate, but give each a primary affiliation for display and faction accounting. Keep office, role and personal relationship as separate variables: a person can be an institutionalist and a Sanchista, or a federalist and a regional power-broker, without the game forcing a false choice.

Each faction should have:
- **Influence:** its practical ability to shape decisions, appointments and party votes.
- **Dissent:** how dissatisfied its members are with Sánchez's leadership and current strategy.
- **Cohesion:** how reliably its members act together. A regional federation or loose network should have lower cohesion than a tightly organised leadership team.
- **Policy priorities:** a small set of positions on investiture, Podemos, Ciudadanos, territorial reform, economic policy and party organisation.
- **Key relationships:** ties to Sánchez, other factions, regional federations, unions and external parties.

Faction influence must not be the same as the number of advisers pinned to the screen. A faction may have few formal advisers but substantial delegate strength, regional office-holders or parliamentary influence. Conversely, a well-represented adviser group may lack support in the Federal Committee.
